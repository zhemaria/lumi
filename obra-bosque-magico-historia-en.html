The Magical Forest · Historical guide

A play by Vicente Cañón Verdasco. The history of fantasy tales as a tool for teaching children: where witches, fairies and curses come from, and why this play teaches without punishing.

At a glance
  • Where they come from: fairy tales were born in the oral tradition. For centuries they were told aloud, and at first they were mostly for adults.
  • How they reached children: between the 17th and 19th centuries, writers such as Perrault, Leprince de Beaumont, the Brothers Grimm and Andersen wrote them down, softened them and gave them an educational purpose.
  • How they teach: the way they teach has changed a great deal, from morals that frightened and punished to stories that invite children to imagine, think and talk.
  • Where The Magical Forest fits: the play uses the classic elements (a curse, witches, fairies, elves, a magic circle), but its lesson is modern. No one gets a terrible punishment: evil is overcome by dancing together, and in the end even the warlocks ask to be friends.
How to use this guide. It is not meant to be read to the children. It helps the teacher know the history of the genre, answer questions that come up at rehearsals with confidence, and use the play to start conversations. The activities are organized by grade.

Timeline
  1. Thousands of years agoPeople tell stories aloud, around the fire and while they work. No one writes them down; each storyteller changes them a little.
  2. 6th century BCE and ancient IndiaThe fables attributed to Aesop, in Greece, and the Panchatantra, in India, use talking animals to teach how to live wisely.
  3. 2nd century CEThe Roman writer Apuleius includes the story of Cupid and Psyche in The Golden Ass, with a mysterious husband and impossible tasks.
  4. Middle AgesIn European villages people tell harsh stories that reflect hunger, illness and the dangers of the forest. They are warnings for survival, not bedtime stories.
  5. 1697In France, Madame d'Aulnoy publishes her Fairy Tales and gives the genre its name. That same year, Charles Perrault publishes his tales, each ending with a moral in verse.
  6. 1756Jeanne-Marie Leprince de Beaumont publishes a short version of Beauty and the Beast written to educate her pupils.
  7. 1762Jean-Jacques Rousseau publishes Emile. The idea grows that childhood is a stage of its own, to be protected and educated.
  8. 1812–1857The Brothers Grimm publish their Children's and Household Tales in Germany. With each edition they soften them further for families.
  9. 1835Hans Christian Andersen publishes his first tales in Denmark. They are his own creations, not collected stories, and they do not always end well.
  10. 1845Heinrich Hoffmann's Struwwelpeter shows the extreme of teaching through fear: terrible punishments for disobedient children.
  11. 1937–1959Disney films (Snow White, Cinderella, Sleeping Beauty) spread a single version of the tales around the world.
  12. 1973Gianni Rodari publishes The Grammar of Fantasy: children do not only listen to stories; they also invent and change them.
  13. 1976Bruno Bettelheim publishes The Uses of Enchantment, about how these stories help children face their fears.
  14. 2019Vicente Cañón Verdasco writes El bosque mágico (The Magical Forest), a play for the whole school to perform.

Who shaped the fairy tale

The classic tales did not come down to us unchanged. Each author reshaped them for their own time and for what they wanted to teach.

  • Madame d'Aulnoy

    France · 1650–1705

    The Blue Bird, The White Cat. She wrote for adults in the Paris salons. Her heroines are clever and make their own decisions.

  • Charles Perrault

    France · 1628–1703

    Little Red Riding Hood, Cinderella, Puss in Boots. He took tales told by country people, wrote them elegantly and added a moral: obey, be careful, be discreet.

  • Jeanne-Marie Leprince de Beaumont

    France · 1711–1780

    Beauty and the Beast. She was a governess. She shortened the tale and used it to teach that kindness matters more than looks.

  • The Brothers Grimm

    Germany · Jacob 1785–1863 · Wilhelm 1786–1859

    Hansel and Gretel, Snow White, Rapunzel. They collected folk tales to preserve German culture. Wilhelm gradually softened them, and in some he turned the mother into a stepmother.

  • Hans Christian Andersen

    Denmark · 1805–1875

    The Ugly Duckling, The Little Mermaid, The Emperor's New Clothes. He invented his own tales. They teach compassion for those who suffer and distrust of the vanity of the powerful.

  • Gianni Rodari

    Italy · 1920–1980

    The Grammar of Fantasy. A teacher and writer, he proposed games for children to invent stories. Imagination, he said, is for everyone: not so that all become artists, but so that no one is a slave.

Why fantasy teaches

Scholars explain in different ways why fantasy tales help children grow. These three ideas are useful when preparing rehearsals.

  • 🧙 Giving fear a face

    According to Bruno Bettelheim, very good and very bad characters help young children sort out their emotions. When fear takes the shape of a witch, it can be looked at, named and overcome, and children learn that courage and help from others can beat what is hard.

  • 🧠 Knowing what others think

    Tales are full of disguises and tricks: the wolf dressed as Grandmother, Gretel fooling the witch. To follow them, children must realize that a character can believe something that is not true. Psychologists call this ability theory of mind, and it is the basis of empathy.

  • 💬 Talking, not preaching

    Today we distinguish between a moral imposed from outside, which children hear as a sermon, and moral education, which happens when children think and share their views about what happened in the story. A lesson works better when it is discovered than when it is dictated.

A useful caution. Scholars such as Maria Tatar and Jack Zipes have criticized Bettelheim for presenting as universal some ideas that belonged to a particular time, such as the passive woman waiting to be rescued. So it helps to choose versions with active characters and to talk with older students about the roles the tales give each character.

The play and the tradition

The Magical Forest is built from very old pieces. This table shows where each one comes from and what the play does with it.

Elements of the play and their origin in traditional tales
In the playIn the traditionWhat the play adds
The narrator tells the story in verse: “Once upon a time…”The oral tale, told aloud, with set phrases to open and close that help listeners remember.It brings back the storyteller and the music of words.
Three villagesThe number three appears in many tales: three siblings, three tasks, three wishes. It creates rhythm and lets listeners predict what comes next.The youngest children recognize the pattern: the scene repeats and the virtue changes.
Witches, warlocks and the cauldronThe witch is one of the oldest and most feared characters in folk tales.They are envious but funny: they make the audience laugh, not shiver.
The curseA spell falls on the characters, like the hundred-year sleep in Sleeping Beauty.It does not take away life or beauty: it takes away joy, friendship and generosity.
Fairies and elvesThe magical helper who appears when the hero cannot succeed alone.They help, but they do not solve it by themselves: they bring a plan for everyone to work together.
The wise peopleThe elders who give advice in folk tales.At first they do not know what to do: looking for solutions is also something we learn.
The circle danceRing dances and rounds, and in European folklore, fairy rings in the forest.The circle brings every grade in the school together: the cure is shared.
The ending: the warlocks ask to be friendsIn the Grimms' tales or in Struwwelpeter, the villain or the disobedient child usually gets a terrible punishment.Here there is no punishment: the warlocks lose their power and are welcomed. It is a modern lesson of reconciliation.
No moral at the endPerrault closed each tale with a moral in verse.The lesson shows through the story and is not dictated: this is Rodari's approach.

Myths and facts

Common ideas about fairy tales worth clearing up, especially with older grades.

Myth: fairy tales were always meant for children.

Fact: for centuries they were told among adults. They were aimed at children once childhood began to be seen as a stage of its own, between the 17th and 19th centuries.

Myth: the Brothers Grimm invented the tales.

Fact: they collected them from the oral tradition and rewrote them. Many were already in circulation, and some, such as Little Red Riding Hood and Cinderella, had been published by Perrault more than a hundred years earlier.

Myth: the tales always had happy endings.

Fact: many old versions were cruel and sad. In Perrault's version, the wolf eats Little Red Riding Hood and no one rescues her. Andersen also wrote sad endings, such as that of The Little Match Girl.

Myth: what a tale teaches is its moral.

Fact: today we believe children learn more from what they experience, feel and discuss in the story than from a sentence added at the end.

Myth: the movies tell the original versions.

Fact: they are one more version, with changes of their own. Every era has told the tales its own way, and comparing versions makes a good classroom activity.

Activities by grade

All are optional. Several use Gianni Rodari's story-inventing games. The “before” activities prepare children for their parts; the “after” activities help close the experience.

  • Pre-K and kindergarten

    Before Make each village's face: joyful, friendly, generous, and then sad. Play a ring dance holding hands.

    After What do you do to make a friend happy? Draw the ice cream you would share, and who you would share it with.

  • 1st and 2nd grade

    Before Read a simple fairy tale and look for pieces that are also in the play: a spell, a magical helper, a happy ending.

    After Rodari's “What would happen if…?” game: what would happen if the fairies had not come? What other solution could the villages find?

  • 3rd and 4th grade

    Before Theory of mind: the audience sees the warlocks hiding in the bushes, but the villages do not. What does the audience know that the characters do not? How does each one feel?

    After Rodari's “fantastic binomial”: draw two words at random (for example, cauldron and bicycle) and invent a group story that connects them.

  • 5th and 6th grade

    Before Compare two versions of the same tale, such as Perrault's and the Grimms' Little Red Riding Hood: what changes, and why? Prepare the narrator's lines paying attention to the rhythm of the verse.

    After Rodari's upside-down tale: rewrite The Magical Forest with kind warlocks and envious fairies. Discussion: is it better to punish someone who does harm, or to give them a chance, as in the play?

Witches and families. In some families, magic and witchcraft can be a sensitive topic. It helps to present the warlocks as what they are in the play: funny characters who make mistakes, lose their power and end up asking to be friends. The cruelest old versions are for the teacher to know about, not to tell the little ones.

To learn more

Classic books on the subject:

  • Gianni Rodari, The Grammar of Fantasy: An Introduction to the Art of Inventing Stories (1973). Games and techniques for inventing stories with children.
  • Bruno Bettelheim, The Uses of Enchantment (1976). The classic psychological view.
  • Jack Zipes and Maria Tatar, several books on the history of fairy tales and their versions.

Online:

  • The tale of Madame d'Aulnoy (Oxford University Press): the writer who gave the genre its name.
  • La construcción de la mente mediante los cuentos de hadas (Redalyc, in Spanish): an article on fairy tales and theory of mind.
  • La herejía de lo macabro (Imaginaria magazine, in Spanish): on Struwwelpeter and teaching through fear.
  • Fairy tale (Wikipedia): an overview of the genre's history and authors.
