Program

INOKA

The Breath of the Prairie and the People of Illinois

A play by José M. Fernández

Welcome

Thank you for joining us. This play, with the whole school taking part, tells the story of the Inoka, the peoples of the prairies and rivers of Illinois, and of their descendants today, the Peoria Tribe of Oklahoma. It is about the land that feeds us when we work together, the rivers that connect the villages, and a council that decides by listening to everyone.

The play

  1. Act I · The Heartbeat of the Prairie and the River · Pre-K & K
    The prairie, the river and their living things, with hand drum and rain sticks.
  2. Act II · The Three Sisters and the Home of Mats · 1st & 2nd
    Growing corn, beans and squash, and houses covered with mats. Song: “Raíz con raíz.”
  3. Act III · The River Routes and Trade Between Villages · 3rd & 4th
    Canoes carved from a single trunk and trade between villages.
  4. Act IV · The Village Assembly and the Wisdom of the Council · 5th & 6th
    Decisions by consensus, the peace calumet and the arrival of the French in 1673.
  5. Act V · The Living Legacy of the Inoka · The whole school
    The Peoria Tribe of Oklahoma today and their language, which is coming back. Closing song: “Legado de la pradera.”

Out of respect for a people who are still here today, no one in this play dresses up as a Native person or uses ceremonial objects: each character is recognized by what they do.

Did you know?

  • Inoka is the name the people called themselves; its meaning is unknown. “Illinois” comes from a word in their language meaning “one who speaks in the ordinary way.”
  • They lived in longhouses and wigwams covered with rush mats, not tipis, and traveled the rivers in canoes carved from a single trunk.
  • The villages were independent and largely decided by consensus.
  • Their descendants are today the Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma, a sovereign nation. Their language, Miami-Illinois, is coming back: children and young people of the Peoria and Miami nations are learning it again.

Thank you

To the Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma, for keeping the history and language of the Inoka alive. To the families, teachers and the whole school staff.
