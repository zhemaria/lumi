Inoka

The Breath of the Prairie and the People of Illinois

A play by José M. Fernández

Before the highways and the brick cities, the Inoka people lived on the prairies and rivers of Illinois. A play for the whole school, from Pre-K to 6th grade, about the land, working together, the council and a people who are still here today: the Peoria Tribe of Oklahoma.

🎬 Production guide: cast, songs, respectful costumes, sets and family letter

📜 Historical guide for teachers: who the Inoka were, myths and facts, and activities by grade

📄 Program · 🖼️ Show poster

This play is about a people who are still here today. Children do not dress up as Native people or use ceremonial objects: each character is recognized by what they do. More details in the production guide.

Act I · The Heartbeat of the Prairie and the River

Stage direction: (Pre-K and K take part: the natural world of Illinois.)

Stage direction: (The stage begins in dim light. We hear the slow, steady beat of a hand drum. At the sides of the stage, 1st and 2nd graders play rain sticks and seed rattles. The Pre-K and K students slowly come in: they are the wind, the prairie flowers, the birds and the bison.)

Narrator 16th

Long before asphalt highways and great brick cities, this wide land of rivers belonged to the open sky, to the tall prairie grasses and to the generous waters of the great Mississippi River.

Narrator 26th

Here, life flowed in balance. The trees sheltered the birds, the grasses fed the bison, and the river brought fresh water to the forests and the valleys. Every living thing was part of a web of respect and reciprocity.

Narrator 16th

In the heart of this land lived families gathered in many villages. French missionaries wrote down that they called themselves “Inoka.”

Narrator 26th

The meaning of the word Inoka was lost in time, but in the stories of the prairie it remains as the memory of a people who lived in harmony with the land and with one another.

Act II · The Three Sisters and the Home of Mats

Stage direction: (1st and 2nd grades take part: farming, houses and working together.)

Stage direction: (The 1st and 2nd graders come in with baskets and with the stalks of the corn, the beans and the squash. Upstage, a longhouse of bent poles covered with rush mats.)

Narrator 16th

In spring and summer, the women of the Inoka villages tended rich gardens in the valleys. Their main food came from three plants that grew together, helping one another, which many peoples today call the Three Sisters.

Corn1st

I am Corn. I grow tall, straight and strong toward the summer sun. With my strong body, I am the pillar and the support for my sisters.

Bean1st

I am Bean. Alone on the ground, I cannot reach up high; but I gently wind around Corn's stalk, climb toward the light and give strength back to the soil.

Squash2nd

I am Squash. I spread my wide leaves over the ground. I keep the soil cool and moist so the drought does not wither my sisters.

Farmer2nd

Our grandmothers understood that the land gives plenty not through force, but through working together. If we plant together, the harvest feeds the whole village.

Weaver2nd

And with the rushes from the marshes we weave the mats that cover our houses. One reed alone breaks when you bend it, but woven all together they protect our families from the wind and the rain.

Chorus1st & 2nd

🎵 Song: “Raíz con raíz”

Raíz con raíz, hojita con hoja,
la tierra florece cuando el pueblo trabaja.
Maíz, calabaza y tierno frijol
protegen el hogar bajo el mismo sol.

What it means: Root by root, little leaf by leaf, / the land blooms when the people work together. / Corn, squash and tender beans / protect the home under the same sun.

Act III · The River Routes and Trade Between Villages

Stage direction: (3rd and 4th grades take part: river travel, trade and alliances between villages.)

Stage direction: (Four students wave the blue cloths like the waters of the Illinois River. Two paddlers move forward inside the cutouts of the dugout canoes, with light wooden paddles.)

Narrator 26th

The rivers of Illinois were not borders, but wide roads for trade. Along them, the villages of the Confederation stayed in touch: the Kaskaskia, the Peoria, the Tamaroa, the Cahokia and, later, allies such as the Michigamea.

Peoria Paddler4th

Greetings, friends from the neighboring village! We travel the river in our canoe, carved from a single tree trunk. We bring dried bison meat and valuable beaver pelts.

Kaskaskia Trader4th

Welcome to our riverbank! Along the rivers and the old trails we trade flint tools, river shells and clay bowls. Trade brings our families together and makes peace stronger.

Narrator 16th

Near these rivers, centuries earlier, the great city of mounds at Cahokia, of the Mississippian culture, had flourished. By the time the Inoka traveled these rivers, that city was already empty. Archaeologists are still studying how that city was related to the peoples who came after.

Village Messenger3rd

(Enters with firm steps, holding a staff of woven reeds.) News from the assembly! The chiefs and the elders of each village are calling us to the meeting of the season. It is time to share what the summer gave us and to listen to the stories of our elders.

Act IV · The Village Assembly and the Wisdom of the Council

Stage direction: (5th and 6th grades take part: decisions by consensus, oral tradition and the arrival of the French.)

Stage direction: (The 5th and 6th graders form a council circle around a symbolic fire. The groups from the other grades sit in an outer half circle: this is the great assembly of the community.)

Keeper of Memory6th

In our villages there was no king and no central government. Each village had its own chiefs: one for peace and one for war, and no one inherited the position. But when big decisions had to be made, we listened to one another until we reached an agreement.

Kaskaskia Council Leader5th

The true strength of a community is not measured by its weapons, but by how well it protects its children, cares for its elders and thinks of those who will come after us.

Peoria Council Leader6th

When visitors came in peace, we offered them the calumet, a ceremonial pipe decorated with feathers. It was our most solemn way of saying: here there is peace, dialogue and hospitality.

Stage direction: (The calumet is mentioned, but it does not appear on stage.)

Narrator 26th

In 1673, the French explorers Jacques Marquette and Louis Jolliet arrived by river. They had already heard about this people from neighboring nations, who called them by a word that sounded like “ilinwe.” That is where the name “Illinois” comes from. And in a Peoria village, they were welcomed with the peace calumet.

Keeper of Memory6th

Marquette believed that “Illinois” meant “the men.” Today, scholars think the word meant “those who speak in the ordinary way.” But the name they gave themselves was a different one: Inoka. And being part of this land calls for maturity, courage to protect the peace and wisdom to share the fire.

Act V · Final Scene: The Living Legacy of the Inoka

Stage direction: (The whole school takes part: from the past to the present of the Peoria Tribe of Oklahoma.)

Stage direction: (The whole cast gradually joins in and takes one step forward, all together.)

Narrator 16th

Centuries went by. The prairies changed their cloak, bridges rose over the rivers, and the villages of mats gave way to the maps of the modern state of Illinois.

Narrator 26th

But the story of the Inoka did not end in the past. They had to leave Illinois: first for Missouri, then for Kansas and, in 1867, for what is now Oklahoma. And there, they carried on.

5th grader5th

Today their descendants form the Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma, a nation recognized by the United States government, which proudly keeps its history and its identity.

3rd grader3rd

Their language, Miami-Illinois, no longer has any native speakers. But children and young people of the Peoria and Miami nations are learning it again, and so the words of their grandparents are heard once more.

1st grader1st

They teach us to care for the rivers, the plants and the forests.

4th grader4th

They teach us that roads are for reaching out a hand and trading in peace.

6th grader6th

And they remind us that, to be worthy of the name Illinois, we must govern ourselves by listening, with fairness and with respect for nature.

All gradesAll

We are the memory of the prairie!
We are the breath of the great river!
We are the present and the legacy of the people of Illinois!

Stage direction: (The hand drums play and the final song begins. The whole cast sings and dances, holding hands.)

All gradesAll

🎵 Song: “Legado de la pradera”

Bajo el cielo abierto creció la hierba alta,
el bisonte, el viento y el agua que no falta.
Maíz, frijol, calabaza, juntitos bajo el sol:
la tierra da sus frutos cuando hay unión.

Pradera dorada, río sin final,
la gente de Illinois vuelve a cantar.
Lo que fue sembrado no se perderá:
¡el aliento sigue, sigue y seguirá!

Por el río bajan canoas de un tronco,
llevan pieles, conchas y un saludo hondo.
En el consejo escuchan, palabra por palabra,
y así nace un acuerdo que a todos ampara.

(Estribillo)

Pasaron los años, el camino fue largo;
dejaron su tierra, pero no su legado.
Hoy en Oklahoma su nación sigue en pie,
y vuelve su lengua: los niños la aprenden también.

(Estribillo, dos veces)

¡Somos el presente y el legado de la gente de Illinois!

What it means: Under the open sky the tall grass grew, the bison, the wind and the water that never runs out. Corn, beans and squash, close together under the sun: the land gives its fruit when there is unity. Chorus: Golden prairie, endless river, the people of Illinois sing again. What was planted will not be lost: the breath goes on and on! Canoes carved from a single trunk come down the river, carrying pelts, shells and a heartfelt greeting. In the council they listen, word by word, and so an agreement is born that protects everyone. The years went by, the road was long; they left their land, but not their legacy. Today their nation still stands in Oklahoma, and their language is coming back: the children are learning it too. We are the present and the legacy of the people of Illinois!
