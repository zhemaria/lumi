The Phoenix on the Lake · Historical guide

A play by José M. Fernández. What teachers need to know about the Great Chicago Fire of 1871 to prepare the children before rehearsals and talk with them after the show.

At a glance
  • What happened: from October 8 to 10, 1871, a fire destroyed downtown Chicago and large parts of the city's South and North Sides.
  • How much: about 3.3 square miles (8.5 km²) and some 17,500 buildings burned. About 300 people died, and between 90,000 and 100,000 lost their homes, nearly a third of a city of about 300,000 people.
  • How it began: in the O'Leary family's barn on DeKoven Street. The cause was never determined.
  • Why it matters: Chicago rebuilt quickly, changed its building rules and, twenty-two years later, welcomed the world to the 1893 Exposition. The phoenix, the bird that rises from its ashes, became a symbol of the city.
How to use this guide. The children don't need to learn all these numbers. The guide is there so teachers can answer confidently the questions that come up in rehearsals. The activities are organized by grade, and the section “The play and the history” explains where the script simplifies.

Timeline
  1. Summer of 1871Drought: from July to early October, barely an inch of rain falls. There are many small fires in the city.
  2. Saturday, October 7A large fire on the West Side burns several blocks. The firefighters end up exhausted, with damaged equipment.
  3. Sunday, October 8, around 8:30 p.m.The fire starts in the O'Leary barn on DeKoven Street. A mistake in locating the flames sends the first firefighters to the wrong place.
  4. After midnightThe southwest wind carries burning pieces of wood over the South Branch of the Chicago River. The fire enters downtown.
  5. Early Monday, October 9The courthouse and city hall burn; its bell falls around 2:30 a.m. The fire crosses the main river to the North Side. The roof of the pumping station burns, and the city's water pipes go dry.
  6. Monday, October 9, all dayThousands of people flee to the shore of Lake Michigan and to the open prairie to the north and west.
  7. Night of the 9th to Tuesday the 10thRain begins. By then the fire had already consumed almost everything in its path; the rain helps put out the last flames.
  8. The following weeksDonations arrive from across the country and abroad. Shelters, food and cleanup are organized.
  9. 1872New rules: downtown buildings must be built with brick, stone and other fire-resistant materials. The Chicago Public Library is founded, with books donated from England.
  10. 1885The Home Insurance Building is completed, considered one of the first skyscrapers with a metal frame.
  11. 1893World's Columbian Exposition: more than 21 million visits. The reporter who invented the cow story admits it wasn't true.
  12. 1917Chicago adopts its flag; one of its red stars stands for the fire.
  13. 1961The Chicago Fire Academy opens on the land where the O'Leary home stood, with the “Pillar of Fire” sculpture marking the spot.
  14. 1997The Chicago City Council declares Catherine O'Leary and her cow innocent.

Why it spread so fast

There was no single cause. Several conditions came together, and it's a good chance for the older students to tell apart the spark that starts something from the conditions that make it grow.

  • 🪵 A city of wood

    More than two-thirds of the buildings were wooden, with tar or wood-shingle roofs. The sidewalks, many streets and the bridges were wooden too.

  • ☀️ The drought

    It had barely rained since July. The wood, the straw and the hay were dry.

  • 🌬️ The wind

    A strong southwest wind pushed the flames and carried embers through the air. Whirls of fire formed that threw burning material blocks away and across the river.

  • 🚒 Tired firefighters

    Chicago had a modern fire department for its time (185 firefighters and 17 horse-drawn steam engines), but they were coming off a week of fires and the big Saturday fire.

  • 📍 A mistake and a delay

    The first alarm gave the wrong location, and the crews were slow to reach the right place.

  • 💧 No water

    When the pumping station roof burned, the pipes stopped carrying water to the hydrants.

The play and the history

A play for children has to simplify. This table shows, act by act, what rests on proven facts and what is worth clarifying in class.

Comparing the script with the historical facts
ActWhat the play saysWhat history says
IThe city was almost all wood; it hadn't rained for weeks; a southwest wind was blowing.Correct. These are the three conditions most often cited.
IDaisy the cow knocks over the lantern.The play presents it as a legend, and that's how to explain it: the reporter who invented it admitted it in 1893. The name “Daisy” comes from the play: there is no record of the cow's name.
IIThe fire crossed the South Branch of the river and then the main river.Correct. The embers crossed through the air, pushed by the wind and the whirls of fire.
IIThe Water Tower is still standing.Correct. It is made of limestone and survived. It wasn't the only one: the pumping station across the street also held up (though its roof burned), as did St. Michael's Church in Old Town and the bell tower of St. James Cathedral.
IIThe rain came on the night of the 9th, and on the 10th the last flames went out.Correct. It's worth adding that the rain didn't “put out the fire” on its own: by then the fire had already burned almost everything it could reach.
IIHundreds dead, about a hundred thousand people homeless, more than seventeen thousand buildings.Correct. About 300 people are estimated to have died, though only about 120 bodies were recovered.
IIIThe sign “All gone but wife, children and energy.”Based on a fact: real estate agent William D. Kerfoot put up a shanty downtown, among the ashes, with a sign like that.
IIIThousands of books arrived from England for the first free public library.Correct. The British donation gave rise to the Chicago Public Library.
IIIDowntown, only stone, brick and iron.In 1872, fire-resistant materials were required within one area of the city. Outside it, people kept building with wood for years.
IIIIn 1885, one of the first skyscrapers with a metal frame.The Home Insurance Building. Historians debate which one was “the first”; the play says “one of the first,” which is the careful choice.
IIIThe flag has four stars; the second stands for the fire and the third for the 1893 Exposition.Correct. The first stands for Fort Dearborn and the fourth for the 1933–1934 Exposition.
IVIn 1893 the reporter confessed, and in 1997 the city declared Mrs. O'Leary innocent.Correct. No one knows for sure how the fire started.

Myths and facts

Children often arrive with one of these ideas. They are a good chance to practice the lesson of Act IV: before you believe a story, look for the evidence.

Myth: Mrs. O'Leary's cow started the fire.

Fact: the story appeared in the newspapers before the flames were out. Reporter Michael Ahern admitted in 1893 that he had made it up. The official investigation of 1871 couldn't determine the cause. A modern historian suggested that a neighbor, Daniel “Peg Leg” Sullivan, may have started the fire, but there is no agreement on that either.

Myth: Chicago had no trained firefighters.

Fact: it had one of the most modern fire departments in the country. The problem was the combination of drought, wind, wood, exhaustion and mistakes.

Myth: the rain put out the fire.

Fact: the rain helped put out the last flames, but the fire had already consumed almost everything within its reach.

Myth: the Water Tower was the only building left standing.

Fact: it's the most famous, but others survived too, like the pumping station across the street and St. Michael's Church.

Myth: it was the deadliest fire in United States history.

Fact: that same night the town of Peshtigo, Wisconsin, burned, and between 1,200 and 2,500 people died there. It is the deadliest fire in the country's history. There were other large fires around Lake Michigan those same days.

The people

  • Catherine O'Leary

    An Irish immigrant who sold milk from her cows in the neighborhood. The newspapers blamed her without evidence, and some cartoons used the story to mock Irish immigrants. She lived the rest of her life with that reputation.

    To discuss Why is it unfair to blame someone without evidence? Why do people sometimes blame whoever is “different”?

  • William D. Kerfoot

    A real estate agent who, days after the fire, opened a small board office among the ruins downtown. His sign became a symbol of the city's spirit.

  • Working families

    Rebuilding was not the same for everyone. Many poor families couldn't afford brick houses and had to move farther from downtown. With older students, you can talk about how a city “rises again,” but not all its people rise again just as fast.

  • The firefighters

    They worked almost without rest from Saturday on. Their experience helped Chicago later build one of the most respected fire departments in the country, with an academy on the very spot where the fire began.

Rebuilding
  • Help from everywhere. Food, clothes, blankets and money arrived from many cities in the country and from other countries. New York and St. Louis sent large sums.
  • Books. More than 8,000 books were donated from Great Britain, and with them the Chicago Public Library was founded, free for everyone.
  • New rules. In 1872, the city required fire-resistant materials downtown. Later it improved its fire department.
  • Architecture. With so much to build, architects and engineers came from all over the country. In the following decades, Chicago became the birthplace of the modern skyscraper.
  • 1893. The World's Columbian Exposition, with its “White City,” showed the world a city that had been in ashes twenty-two years before.
  • The phoenix. In mythology, the phoenix is a bird that burns and rises again from its ashes. From the very days of the fire, newspapers and illustrations compared Chicago to a phoenix rising from the ashes.

Activities by grade

All are optional. The “before” activities help the children understand their part; the “after” activities help close the experience.

  • Pre-K and kindergarten

    Before Show photos of wooden houses and brick houses: which one burns more easily? Practice the chant with claps.

    After Practice the school's fire drill: walk, don't run, and follow the adult. Draw the phoenix flying.

  • 1st and 2nd grade

    Before Read aloud a simple account of the fire and find the river and the lake on a map of Chicago.

    After Make a class list, “What we do if there's smoke,” and take it home to talk about the family escape plan.

  • 3rd and 4th grade

    Before Read the 3rd grade story about the Great Fire in English or Spanish.

    After Put five moments from the play in order on a timeline. Explain the three conditions with drawings: wood, drought and wind.

  • 5th and 6th grade

    Before Separate “legend” from “fact”: read the cow story and look for what evidence there is. Tell apart the spark (how it started) from the conditions (why it grew).

    After A short debate: why did people blame Catherine O'Leary? Who benefited most from the rebuilding, and who least? Compare the Chicago flag with the facts in the play.

Safety first. Some children may have lived through a fire or be afraid of fire. It helps to present the play as the story of how the city learned to protect itself and rose again, and to keep the Fire Chief's safety rules always in view.
