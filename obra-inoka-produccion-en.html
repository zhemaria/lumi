Cultural respect comes first

This play is about a people who are still here today. The descendants of the Inoka are today the Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma, a nation recognized by the United States government. The play presents them with respect, without movie stereotypes.

Five rules for the whole production

  1. No one dresses up as a Native person. No feathers, headdresses, fringe, imitation necklaces or face paint. Each character is recognized by what they do and what they carry (see costumes).
  2. Sacred objects are not made or used on stage. The calumet is mentioned in Act IV, but it does not appear. No water drums or other ceremonial objects are used.
  3. No imitation of chants, war cries or ways of speaking. The children speak in their own voices. The music uses hand drums, rain sticks, rattles and flute, without copying any people's songs.
  4. Use the present tense when it applies. The Inoka did not “disappear”: Act V says so clearly, and it is worth repeating in class.
  5. Say the names correctly. Inoka, Kaskaskia, Peoria, Tamaroa, Cahokia, Michigamea, Miami-Illinois. See the historical guide.
Before a public performance, it is a good courtesy to write to the Peoria Tribe to tell them about the project and offer them the script. Their contact information is at peoriatribe.com.

At a glance
  • What it's about: the prairie and rivers of Illinois, the Inoka villages and their way of life: growing corn, beans and squash, houses covered with mats, trade along the rivers, and the council that decides by agreement. It ends in the present, with the Peoria Tribe of Oklahoma and their language, which is coming back.
  • Running time: 20 to 25 minutes, with the two songs.
  • Cast: the whole school. Each act belongs to two grades; Act V brings everyone together.
  • Languages: the script is in Spanish and English, with the same lines. Both songs are sung in Spanish.

Music and songs

Instruments: hand drum, rain sticks, seed rattles and flute. Students can play them. No recordings of Native ceremonial songs are used.

🎵 «Raíz con raíz»

Act II · 1st and 2nd grade chorus, after the Weaver.

Lyrics (sung in Spanish)

Raíz con raíz, hojita con hoja,
la tierra florece cuando el pueblo trabaja.
Maíz, calabaza y tierno frijol
protegen el hogar bajo el mismo sol.

Root by root, little leaf by leaf, / the land blooms when the people work together. / Corn, squash and tender beans / protect the home under the same sun.

🎵 «Legado de la pradera»

Closing song · The whole cast, after the chorus “We are the memory of the prairie!”.

Lyrics (sung in Spanish)

Bajo el cielo abierto creció la hierba alta,
el bisonte, el viento y el agua que no falta.
Maíz, frijol, calabaza, juntitos bajo el sol:
la tierra da sus frutos cuando hay unión.

Estribillo:
Pradera dorada, río sin final,
la gente de Illinois vuelve a cantar.
Lo que fue sembrado no se perderá:
¡el aliento sigue, sigue y seguirá!

Por el río bajan canoas de un tronco,
llevan pieles, conchas y un saludo hondo.
En el consejo escuchan, palabra por palabra,
y así nace un acuerdo que a todos ampara.

(Estribillo)

Pasaron los años, el camino fue largo;
dejaron su tierra, pero no su legado.
Hoy en Oklahoma su nación sigue en pie,
y vuelve su lengua: los niños la aprenden también.

(Estribillo, dos veces)

¡Somos el presente y el legado de la gente de Illinois!

What it means: Under the open sky the tall grass grew, the bison, the wind and the water that never runs out. Corn, beans and squash, close together under the sun: the land gives its fruit when there is unity. Chorus: Golden prairie, endless river, the people of Illinois sing again. What was planted will not be lost: the breath goes on and on! Canoes carved from a single trunk come down the river, carrying pelts, shells and a heartfelt greeting. In the council they listen, word by word, and so an agreement is born that protects everyone. The years went by, the road was long; they left their land, but not their legacy. Today their nation still stands in Oklahoma, and their language is coming back: the children are learning it too. We are the present and the legacy of the people of Illinois!

Both songs were written for this play and are sung in Spanish. Each line is sung twice, so the little ones can repeat it.

Sets and props
  • The prairie (fixed backdrop): kraft paper or canvas with tall golden grasses, wildflowers and open sky.
  • The rivers: two light cloths, royal blue and turquoise, 20 to 26 feet long, gently waved by students.
  • The canoes: reinforced cardboard cutouts, painted like carved and burned wood, with straps to carry them at waist height. Inoka canoes were carved from a single tree trunk.
  • The longhouse: flexible arches (PVC pipes or poles) covered with straw, jute or reed mats. In summer the Inoka lived in longhouses shared by several families; in winter, in smaller wigwams. Both were covered with rush mats.
  • The council fire: symbolic, made of orange paper or cloth. Never real fire.

Rehearsals

Weeks 1 to 3: each group in its classroom

  • Pre-K and K (15 min): come in with the music, moving like the wind, the animals and the flowers, and “freeze” in a half circle.
  • 1st and 2nd (20 min): their lines, “Raíz con raíz,” and moving the stalks and mats; the musicians, the beat of Act I.
  • 3rd and 4th (25 min): moving the cloths and the canoes; the messenger's, paddler's and trader's lines.
  • 5th and 6th (30 min): projecting their voices, the pauses of the council, and the narration.
  • Everyone: “Legado de la pradera.”

Weeks 4 and 5: act by act

Each act with its music, entrances and exits from the sides. Act V, with everyone, at the end of week 5.

Last week

Two or three full run-throughs with props and songs.

No one has to memorize if they can't. Narrators can read from a script in a black folder. In the interactive script, each child can choose a part, see only their own lines and listen to them.
