At a glance
  • Who: the Inoka, whom the French called “Illinois.” They were a confederation of about twelve allied tribes or villages, including the Kaskaskia, Peoria, Cahokia, Tamaroa and, from around 1700, part of the Michigamea.
  • Where: the prairies and river valleys of the Illinois and Mississippi rivers, in what is now Illinois and nearby areas.
  • How they lived: in spring and summer, in large villages by the rivers, where women grew corn, beans and squash; in winter, in smaller hunting camps. Men hunted bison on the prairie.
  • Today: their descendants are the Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma, headquartered in Miami, Oklahoma.

The name: Inoka and Illinois

Inoka is the name the people called themselves, as French missionaries wrote down. Its meaning is unknown.

Illinois comes from a word in their own language, Miami-Illinois, meaning roughly “one who speaks in the ordinary way.” The French first heard it from other peoples of the Great Lakes region, who used it for the Inoka, and wrote it as “Illinois.”

Father Marquette believed “Illinois” meant “the men,” and later writers added “the complete men” or “the complete people.” They are beautiful, but they do not hold up. In the play, “the men” appears as what it was: Marquette's interpretation.

Timeline
  • About 1050–1350Cahokia, the great city of mounds of the Mississippian culture, flourishes near present-day St. Louis. It began to empty after 1200 and was nearly abandoned by about 1350.
  • 1600sThe Inoka live in villages in the Illinois River valley and on both sides of the Mississippi.
  • 1673Jacques Marquette and Louis Jolliet travel down the Mississippi. In a Peoria village, they are welcomed with the peace calumet. On their way back, they pass the Grand Village of the Kaskaskia, near Starved Rock.
  • 1675Marquette founds a mission at the Grand Village of the Kaskaskia.
  • Around 1700Part of the Michigamea, who lived in northeastern Arkansas, join the Kaskaskia.
  • After 1763Part of the people move to Missouri.
  • 1832By treaty, the Peoria and Kaskaskia give up their last lands and receive land in Kansas, near the Piankashaw and Wea.
  • 1854The Peoria, Kaskaskia, Piankashaw and Wea formally unite as one tribe.
  • 1867They move to Indian Territory, today Oklahoma.
  • 1959The federal government ends its relationship with the tribe, as it did with many others at that time.
  • 1978Congress restores federal recognition to the Peoria Tribe.
  • TodayThe Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma is a sovereign nation with its own government, and it is working to bring its language back.

Life in the villages

Houses

In the summer villages, families lived in longhouses: frames of bent poles, up to about 58 feet long, where several families lived around shared hearths. In winter they used smaller, dome-shaped wigwams. Both were covered with rush mats woven by the women; a double layer kept out rain and snow. They did not live in tipis.

Farming and hunting

Women grew corn, the main food, along with beans and squash, and gathered wild plants, nuts, berries and honey. The three plants help one another: corn supports the beans, beans enrich the soil, and squash leaves keep it moist. Many peoples today call them the “Three Sisters,” a name that comes from Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) tradition.

Men hunted, above all bison on the prairie, and took care of warfare. In late summer and winter, the villages split into smaller groups that went to hunting camps.

Rivers and trade

Rivers were roads. The Inoka traveled in canoes carved from a single tree trunk (dugouts), not bark canoes, and traded pelts, dried meat, stone tools, shells and pottery with other villages and peoples.

Government and the calumet

There was no king and no central government. Each village had its own chiefs: peace chiefs, who organized hunts and relations with other peoples, and war chiefs. These positions were not simply inherited: they depended on earned respect, and chiefs led by persuasion, not force. Important decisions were largely made by consensus, listening to elders and clans.

The calumet was a ceremonial pipe. Offering it was a solemn way to welcome visitors and seal peace; whoever carried it could travel safely. Because it is a sacred object, the play mentions it but does not show it on stage.

Myths, facts and open questions

Myth: “Illinois (or Illiniwek) means ‘the complete men.’”

Fact: that came from later interpretations of Marquette. The word means roughly “one who speaks in the ordinary way.” The people's own name, Inoka, has an unknown meaning.

Myth: “They lived in tipis and wore big feather headdresses.”

Fact: they lived in longhouses and wigwams covered with rush mats. The headdresses in movies come from some peoples of the Great Plains, where they have a ceremonial meaning.

Open question: “Did the Inoka build the city of Cahokia?”

What we know: Cahokia belongs to the Mississippian culture and flourished centuries before 1673. The site was named after the Cahokia, a tribe of the Confederation that lived nearby in colonial times. The Peoria Tribe sees itself as descended from the peoples who built the great mounds; archaeologists are still studying that connection.

Myth: “They had a king or a great chief who ruled everyone.”

Fact: they were allied, independent villages with peace and war chiefs, which largely decided by consensus.

Myth: “The Native peoples of Illinois disappeared.”

Fact: their descendants are today the Peoria Tribe of Oklahoma, a federally recognized nation.

The Peoria Tribe today

The Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma brings together descendants of the Peoria, Kaskaskia, Piankashaw and Wea. It has its own government, elected by its citizens, and its headquarters in Miami, Oklahoma.

Their language, Miami-Illinois, no longer has native speakers, but the Peoria and Miami nations are working to bring it back, and today children and young people are learning it again.

Official website: peoriatribe.com.

Activities by grade

  • Pre-K and K

    The prairie moves. Listen to the drum and move like the wind, the flowers, the birds and the bison: slowly, quickly, frozen.

    Count how many prairie animals and plants they know.

  • 1st and 2nd

    Three sisters in a cup. Plant a corn seed, a bean seed and a squash seed in a clear cup and watch them grow.

    Weave a mat with paper strips, and test that the woven mat is stronger than a single strip.

  • 3rd and 4th

    Rivers are roads. On a map of Illinois, find the Illinois and Mississippi rivers, Starved Rock and Peoria Lake.

    Barter: trade classroom objects without money and talk about what makes a trade fair.

  • 5th and 6th

    Decide by consensus. Make a class decision by listening to everyone until there is agreement, and compare it with a vote.

    Sources: compare what the French wrote in 1673 with what the Peoria Tribe says today on its website. Who tells the story?

Talking about this topic with respect
  • Talk about specific peoples (Inoka, Peoria, Kaskaskia) rather than “Indians” in general.
  • Use the present tense when it applies: the Peoria Tribe exists today.
  • Do not ask children to dress up, imitate war cries or make sacred objects as a “craft.”
  • If there are children of Native heritage in the class, from any people, let them share only if they want to.
